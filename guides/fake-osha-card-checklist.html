Fake OSHA card checklist

You cannot authenticate a wallet card by hunting for holograms. You authenticate it the way OSHA says: trainer records or the plastic QR, then the OTI Education Center.

A worker hands over an OSHA 10. The print looks cheap, or the story about the class does not. Your job is not to play document examiner. Your job is to use OSHA’s published checks — and to refuse “verification websites” that OSHA does not acknowledge.

Facts below are from the OSHA Outreach FAQ. FieldCred stores the file you upload and can stamp an admin attestation. It does not detect counterfeits by looking at a photo, and it has no OSHA API.

Stop: these are not verification

  • National serial-number websites. OSHA does not operate, maintain, or acknowledge any national database to verify plastic or paper student course completion cards. A site that claims otherwise is not OSHA, no matter how official the logo looks.
  • Holograms, DOL watermarks, microprint “tells.” OSHA does not tell employers to authenticate cards that way. Counterfeiters copy decoration. Do not teach your gate to trust it.
  • Calling OSHA and asking them to look up a student. For privacy reasons, OSHA does not provide individual verification. Verification is at the cardholder’s discretion.
  • Treating the card as a license. It is a student course completion card — not a certification, not a license, and not standard-specific training.

Checks that actually mean something

  1. Who taught the class? Get the authorized trainer’s name. Trainers keep records for five years. If they cannot confirm the student, the class type, and the date, you do not have a verified card.
  2. Plastic card — scan the back. The QR on the back of a plastic Outreach card points you to the OTI Education Center that processed it. That center verifies. The QR is not a public national registry.
  3. Could this trainer even issue cards? Before class — and when a card looks off — ask for the trainer’s current Authorized Trainer card (name, authorization expiration, Authorizing Training Organization). Call the ATO listed on it.
  4. Timing. Trainers must issue the student card within 90 days of class. “I finished yesterday, here’s the official OSHA card” is the wrong story. A same-day certificate from the trainer is a courtesy; acceptance of that in lieu of the student card is the employer’s or jobsite’s call, not OSHA’s.
  5. Replacement story. Replacements come only from the original trainer, only within five years, only once per student per class. OSHA cannot replace a card. A third party selling “replacements” is a red flag.
  6. What was sold. Outreach is voluntary at the federal level. It does not meet OSHA standard-specific training. A seller who promises the 10-hour class “covers your OSHA training requirements” is overselling.
If you believe the training or the card is fraudulent Email outreach@dol.gov or call the outreach fraud line at 847-725-7804. Include trainer name, date, location, class type (Construction / General Industry / Maritime / Disaster Site; 10 or 30 hours), and what you saw. OSHA keeps the reporter confidential.

At the gate, keep it operational

A fake-looking card is a delay, not a debate. Hold the worker, call the safety manager, and run the trainer or OTI check. Do not wave someone through because the plastic “looks right.”

If this bounce is a staffing dispatch, use the gate-rejection playbook so the next body through the gate already has a public QR and a file that matches the card.

What to keep on file

  • Photo or PDF of both sides of the card (QR lives on the back of plastic cards).
  • Trainer name and ATO, plus the date you called.
  • Card number as printed — it is already on the physical badge, so showing it on a public record page is not a new exposure.
  • In FieldCred: the admin attestation after a real lookup. See how to verify an OSHA 10.
