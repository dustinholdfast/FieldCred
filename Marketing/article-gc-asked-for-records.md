# The GC Asked for Your Crew's Training Records — Here's What to Send

*SEO target: "GC asked for training records," "certification records for jobsite," "what does a GC check before mobilization"*

It usually arrives as an email, a week or two before mobilization. The general contractor — maybe one you've worked with for years — wants documentation for every worker you're sending: OSHA cards, licenses, operator certs, site orientation, sometimes drug-screen and background confirmations. There's a spreadsheet attached and a deadline that feels aggressive.

First: this is normal, and it's not aimed at you. But it's also not busywork you can half-answer. Here's why the request showed up, what they're actually checking, and how to respond without turning your office upside down for three days.

## Why the GC is asking

The GC owns the site and, as far as their client and their insurer are concerned, everything that happens on it. Owners are pushing prequalification requirements down, insurers are pricing off documented safety programs, and one uncertified operator on an incident report is a problem that lands on the GC first.

So GCs verify up front. The records request is partly a paperwork exercise and partly an audition: they're quietly sorting subs into "send them work" and "watch them closely." Slow, incomplete, or padded responses put you in the second pile — and the sub that answers same-day with clean records is telling the GC something about how they run everything else.

## What they're actually checking

**OSHA 10 / OSHA 30.** The baseline safety training cards — OSHA 10 for workers, OSHA 30 typically for foremen and supervisors. Many GCs and several states require them for anyone on site. The card doesn't technically expire under federal OSHA, but many GCs and jurisdictions want cards issued within the last five years. Know which standard your GC applies before you argue.

**Trade licenses.** Electricians, plumbers, and other licensed trades — current state or local license, matched to the person actually doing the work, not just the company qualifier.

**Operator certifications.** Cranes, forklifts, aerial lifts, rigging. These *do* expire — forklift certs every three years, for example — and they're the most common thing to lapse quietly, because the wallet card is in a truck somewhere and nobody tracks the date.

**Site-specific items.** Orientation, badging, drug-screen confirmations, sometimes medical fit or background checks depending on the owner. These are per-site, which is why last project's paperwork doesn't carry over.

## The mistake that costs subs the relationship

The instinct under deadline is to send everything you can find and hope it covers the list. Don't. GCs notice three things immediately: cards that expired, names on certs that don't match the roster you submitted, and gaps papered over with "we'll get you that one later."

An honest partial answer beats a padded complete-looking one. "Eighteen of twenty are attached; two operators recertify Thursday and I'll send those cards Friday morning" keeps you in the first pile. A stack that quietly includes an expired card puts you in the second — and the GC's compliance person, unlike you, has software that catches it.

## How to answer in hours instead of days

**1. Confirm the exact list and format first.** One email: which credentials, for which workers, in what format, by when. Guessing at the requirements is how you do this twice.

**2. Build the roster before you pull documents.** Who is actually mobilizing? Chasing certs for workers who aren't going wastes the time you don't have.

**3. Check dates before you send, not after they do.** Every card gets a ten-second look: is it current, and does the name match the roster? Anything expired goes on the renewals list, not in the packet.

**4. Flag the gaps yourself, with dates attached.** A gap with a plan reads as competence. A gap they discover reads as a warning.

**5. Fix the system, not just this request.** If this took three days of digging through binders, phone photos, and a spreadsheet nobody trusts — the next request will too. The subs who answer same-day aren't luckier; they keep every worker's credentials in one tracked place with expirations flagged before they lapse.

## What happens if you shrug it off

Nothing dramatic — and that's the trap. Nobody calls to yell. Your crew might even get on site this time. But the GC's prequal file now says slow-and-incomplete, and when the next project staffs up, the invitations quietly go to the subs who made verification easy. For most contractors, that's the real stake: not a fine, not an inspection — the work going somewhere else.
